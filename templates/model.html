The Model — full specification

Every equation behind the Edge, at the live product configuration. The model is price-only: it ranks stocks on their past returns. No fundamentals are used to pick names.

1 Universe & eligibility

At each rebalance date \(d\), keep US names whose point-in-time market cap clears the size floor. Delisted companies remain in the history and can be held into their delisting.

$$\operatorname{mcap}_i(d)\ \ge\ \$10\text{B}$$

2 Signal (price only)

12-1 momentum — the twelve-month return, skipping the most recent month. \(P_i(t)\) is the split- and dividend-adjusted close.

$$\text{mom}_i(t)=\frac{P_i(t-21)}{P_i(t-251)}-1$$

Skipping the last month is deliberate: very recent moves tend to partly reverse, so including them works against the signal.

3 Cross-sectional score

Standardise the signal across all eligible names on that date, so the ranking is relative to the field that day rather than to an absolute return level.

$$s_i=\frac{\text{mom}_i-\mu}{\sigma}$$

4 Selection — top 10, capped by sector

Walk the ranking best-first, admitting a name only while its sector holds fewer than \(c=2\) names. A 10-name book therefore spans at least five sectors.

$$\text{admit } i \iff \#\{j\in B:\ \text{sector}_j=\text{sector}_i\}

5 Execution, weighting & the two risk overlays

Signals are formed at the close of day \(t\); the trade enters at the close of \(t+1\). Positions are equal-weight. Two independent overlays then scale exposure, because they see different dangers: the regime gate \(g\) watches the market, and the volatility target \(v\) watches the book itself.

$$w_i=\tfrac1n,\qquad g(d)=\begin{cases}1 & \text{S\&P}(d)\ \ge\ \text{MA}_{200}(d)\\[2pt] 0.25 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

The gate is evaluated every day, not frozen at the rebalance, so a market that breaks its average mid-holding de-risks immediately rather than up to two months later.

The volatility target scales toward \(\sigma^\ast=25\%\) annualized using the portfolio's own trailing 21-day volatility. It is capped at 1, so the model de-levers but never borrows, and \(\hat\sigma\) is measured through \(d-1\) — the scale applied today cannot peek at today's return.

$$\hat\sigma(d)=\sqrt{252}\cdot\text{sd}\big(r_p(d-21..d-1)\big),\qquad v(d)=\min\!\left(1,\ \frac{\sigma^\ast}{\hat\sigma(d)}\right)$$
$$e(d)=g(d)\,v(d),\qquad r_p(d)=e(d)\sum_i w_i\,r_i(d)\;+\;\big(1-e(d)\big)\,r_f\;-\;c\,\big|\Delta v(d)\big|$$

The final term charges the same 10bps to re-levering that a rebalance pays: changing exposure is itself a trade, and a risk control that traded for free would look better than it is.

6 Transaction costs → net return

Turnover \(\tau\) is the share of the book replaced at a rebalance; each unit costs 10 bps.

$$\tau_k=1-\frac{|B_k\cap B_{k-1}|}{|B_k|},\qquad r^{\text{net}}=r^{\text{gross}}-0.0010\,\tau_k$$

7 Reported metrics

Measured on the daily equity curve, so drawdown is the true intra-period peak-to-trough rather than a sampled approximation.

$$\text{CAGR}=E_T^{252/T}-1,\quad \text{Sharpe}=\frac{\sqrt{252}\,\bar r}{\sigma_r},\quad \text{maxDD}=\min_t\!\left(\frac{E_t}{\max_{u\le t}E_u}-1\right)$$

In plain English

What the model is trying to do

It bets that stocks which have risen over the past year tend to keep rising for a while. That pattern is called momentum and it is one of the most studied effects in finance. The model reads no news, earnings reports or opinions — only past prices.

What it does, step by step

  • Start with big companies only. Each must be worth at least $10 billion. Smaller companies were tested and the strategy got steadily worse the further down it went, so the size floor is part of the model rather than a convenience.
  • Measure each one's past year of returns, ignoring the most recent month.
  • Rank them against each other on that date. What matters is beating the other candidates that day, not clearing a fixed number.
  • Buy the top 10 — but never more than 2 from the same sector. Without that cap the model once held 9 technology stocks out of 10, which is really one bet wearing ten badges.
  • Split the money evenly across the 10 and hold for about two months before re-picking.
  • Step mostly aside when the market is falling. If the S&P 500 is below its 200-day average, the model holds only 25% invested and the rest in cash. Momentum earns in rising markets and suffers in falling ones, so this rule exists to avoid the worst of that. It checks this every day, not just on re-pick day — otherwise a market that turned down could be ridden all the way to the bottom of a two-month holding period.
  • Shrink the position size when the portfolio itself gets jumpy. Separately from what the market is doing, the model measures how violently its own holdings have been moving over the last month. If that is running hotter than a 25%-a-year pace, it scales the whole book down proportionally and parks the difference in cash. This exists because of a specific hole in the rule above: the two worst losses in the model's history happened while the S&P was above its 200-day average and falling only about 10%. The market was fine; the momentum stocks were not. A rule that only watches the market cannot see that coming, but the portfolio's own turbulence can.

What each parameter means

  • $10B floor — the smallest company it will consider.
  • 12-1 momentum — the ranking rule: last 12 months' return, skipping the last month.
  • 10 names, equal weight — 10% of the money in each position.
  • Sector cap 2 — at most 2 of the 10 from any one industry.
  • Monthly clock — it re-picks on the first trading day of every month, and each book is held until the next one replaces it. The date is a product choice: a book has to arrive on a day subscribers can plan around. It is not the highest-scoring clock — the 2-month version backtests better — so the cost of it is stated in the results below rather than hidden.
  • t+1 execution — it buys at the next day's close, because you cannot rank every stock using a closing price and then also trade at that same price.
  • 10 bps costs — 0.10% charged on the portion of the book that changes, approximating real trading friction.
  • 25% in a downtrend — the exposure kept while the market is below its 200-day average, checked daily rather than only when the book is re-picked.
  • 25% volatility target — the pace of movement the model tries to hold the portfolio to. If its recent swings imply a faster pace, it invests proportionally less. It can only scale down: it never borrows to bet more than 100%.
  • 21-day volatility window — how far back it looks to judge that pace. It deliberately uses only days that have already finished, so it is never reacting to a move it has not lived through yet.

How the backtest was built, and why that matters

A backtest simulates what these rules would have done in the past. It is very easy to make one look good by accident, so three things were done deliberately:

  • Dead companies are included. The data covers 12,164 companies, 7,851 of which no longer exist. About a quarter of all positions the simulation ever took were in companies that later went bankrupt or were acquired. Testing only on companies that survived to today is the most common way a backtest lies — it inflated an earlier version of this model by roughly 8 percentage points a year.
  • Nothing uses information from the future. Prices and company data are used only from dates on which they were genuinely public.
  • Results are measured daily. An earlier round of our own testing sampled every two months and reported a 16% worst-case loss for a portfolio whose real worst-case loss was 48%. Sampling that coarsely cannot see a crash that happens and recovers in between.

What this model does not know

  • It has no idea what any company does, or whether it earns money. A stock that rose for a year is bought whether the business is excellent or terrible.
  • It cannot anticipate a crash. Both safety rules react after trouble has started — the 200-day rule after the market has turned down, the volatility rule after the portfolio has already had a rough month. They limit damage rather than avoiding it.
  • Ten stocks is concentrated. Individual positions can and do fall heavily.
  • Scaling down has a price. In a sharp fall followed by a fast rebound, the model is holding cash exactly when the recovery starts, and it re-invests only after the calm has lasted long enough to show up in the measurement. Smoother is not the same as better.
Honest limits. The historical simulation returned about 17.5% a year against the S&P 500's 8.7%, with a worst-case loss of 46% (the index itself lost 55% in 2008). The volatility target is a deliberate trade — give up some annual return to cut the worst case — not a free improvement. Those are simulated results on past data, and the rules were chosen knowing how that period turned out. The previous version of this model — a different momentum rule — was retired in July 2026 after testing on survivorship-free data showed it lost to the index. Real forward-looking evidence begins the day the current rules went live and is shown on the Edge Tracker page. None of this is investment advice.
What we corrected on 30 July 2026. Until this date this page claimed 18.3% a year and a worst-case loss of 31%. Both were too good — and not because of a coding error, but because of a choice nobody realised they were making. The model re-picks on a fixed cycle, and that cycle has to start somewhere. The start date had been inherited from an unrelated piece of setup and was never questioned. When we finally tested it — same rules, same data, just starting the cycle one week later, then two weeks, and so on — the worst-case loss came out at 44%, 43%, 45%, 48% and 48%. The 31% was the best of six, and the only one that got out of the 2021–23 downturn early. That was luck with a start date, presented as a property of the model. The cycle is now pinned to the first trading day of every month, so there is no start date left to get lucky with, and the figures above are what that actually produces.
